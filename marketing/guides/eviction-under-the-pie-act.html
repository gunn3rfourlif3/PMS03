Eviction under the PIE Act: the realistic timeline, and the mistakes that restart it

The landlord wants a number, and you cannot honestly give one — which is the first thing to explain to them. What you can give is the sequence, because an eviction that runs cleanly through the Act's steps is slow, and one that skips a step is slower, because it generally starts again.

Your tenant is not a squatter, and the Act still applies

The Prevention of Illegal Eviction from and Unlawful Occupation of Land Act 19 of 1998 — PIE — reads as though it were written about land invasions, and principals routinely assume it has nothing to do with a tenant who simply stopped paying. It does. Section 1 defines an unlawful occupier as a person occupying land without the owner's or person in charge's express or tacit consent, and without any other right in law to occupy it. Once a lease is validly terminated that consent is gone, and the definition fits.

The Supreme Court of Appeal settled this in Ndlovu v Ngcobo; Bekker and Another v Jika [2002] ZASCA 87, handed down on 30 August 2002: PIE applies to a person whose occupation was lawful at the outset and has since become unlawful, so an owner evicting a tenant holding over must follow the Act's procedure. There is no shorter common-law route: section 4(1) applies despite any other law or the common law, and section 8(1) permits eviction only on a court order. The definition does exclude occupiers under the Extension of Security of Tenure Act 62 of 1997, which governs farm land and dwellings, so confirm which Act applies if a property is agricultural.

Before PIE, the termination has to be sound

Almost every eviction that fails, fails here, before the Act is reached: the court will ask whether this person is an unlawful occupier, which depends entirely on whether the lease actually ended. For a fixed-term lease where the tenant is a natural person, section 14 of the Consumer Protection Act 68 of 2008 is the provision that catches agencies. Section 14(2)(b)(ii) allows the supplier to cancel 20 business days after written notice of a material failure the consumer has not remedied, and section 14(1) excludes only transactions between juristic persons — so an ordinary residential lease to an individual is inside it, whatever the lease says. In Centpret Properties (Pty) Ltd v Shandukane [2023] JOL 61090 (GJ) the lease allowed seven days to remedy; the court held the tenant could insist on the statutory 20. Section 14 governs fixed-term agreements only, so a lease running month to month after its term expired falls outside it.

Then there is the ground you chose. Molusi and Others v Voges N.O. and Others [2016] ZACC 6, decided on 1 March 2016, was an Extension of Security of Tenure Act matter rather than a PIE one, but its pleading point travels. The termination notices cited non-payment of rent; by argument the landowner was relying instead on a need to develop the land. The Constitutional Court held that relying on grounds neither stated in the notice nor pleaded was impermissible, and set the eviction aside.

A timing trap sits alongside this. Under section 13(7) of the Rental Housing Act 50 of 1999, from the date a tenant lodges a Rental Housing Tribunal complaint until the Tribunal rules or three months pass, whichever is earlier, the landlord may not evict — so a complaint lodged after your demand can hold the position for up to three months, though the Tribunal cannot itself evict.

The sequence the Act requires

PIE requires two separate services, in a specific order, and that is where it departs from an ordinary application. Proceedings are instituted under section 4(1) by the owner or person in charge — a person with legal authority to give permission to enter or reside on the land. Papers are then served and filed as the rules of court prescribe, under section 4(3).

Separately, section 4(2) requires written and effective notice of the proceedings on both the unlawful occupier and the municipality having jurisdiction, at least 14 days before the hearing. Section 4(5) prescribes its contents: that proceedings are being instituted under section 4(1), the date and time of the hearing, the grounds for the eviction, and the occupier's right to appear, defend and apply for legal aid where necessary.

The step most often missed is that this notice is not yours to issue. In Cape Killarney Property Investments (Pty) Ltd v Mahamba and Others [2001] ZASCA 87, decided on 10 September 2001, the SCA held that its contents and the manner of service must be authorised and directed by an order of the court, and that issue by a registrar or clerk does not suffice. Because the notice carries the hearing date, which can only be fixed once the main papers have been served, those directions are sought afterwards — in practice by an ex parte application supported by the return of service. The 14 days run from service of that directed notice, not from your demand.

At the hearing the court applies section 4(6) where occupation has been shorter than six months and section 4(7) where it is longer, the second adding an enquiry into whether alternative land is available or can reasonably be made available. Both require the court to be of the opinion that eviction is just and equitable, having considered all relevant circumstances including the needs of the elderly, children, disabled persons and households headed by women. If it grants eviction, section 4(8) requires a just and equitable date to vacate and a later date after which the order may be executed — so the warrant is a further step.

Why no honest guide gives you a total duration

Nothing in PIE sets an outer limit. The Act fixes one period — the 14 days in section 4(2) — and leaves the rest to the rules of court, the roll of the particular court, and the court's discretion on dates under sections 4(8) and 4(9). Add the 20 business days before cancellation where section 14 applies, two rounds of sheriff's service with an ex parte application between them, and a vacate date the court considers just and equitable: you can see the shape without being able to total it. Two things stretch it. Where homelessness is a real risk the local authority must be joined, its duty to provide temporary emergency accommodation being relevant. And under section 18 of the Superior Courts Act 10 of 2013 an application for leave to appeal, or an appeal, suspends the operation and execution of the order — so a defended matter can stall after you have won it.

The mistakes that send you back to the start

Four recur. Serving the section 4(2) notice without court directions, which Cape Killarney puts beyond argument. Incorporating the grounds by reference — saying they appear in the affidavit rather than setting them out in the notice, which does not meet section 4(5)(c). Cancelling the lease before the statutory notice period has run, which leaves you without an unlawful occupier. And treating agreement as a shortcut: in Occupiers of Erven 87 and 88 Berea v De Wet N.O. [2017] ZACC 18, decided on 8 June 2017, the Constitutional Court held that a purported consent does not relieve the court of forming its own view on whether eviction is just and equitable, and that where information about the occupiers is missing it must refuse the order.

A fifth is not procedural but criminal, usually a frustrated landlord acting alone at month nine. Changing the locks or cutting water or electricity to force a tenant out is an eviction within the Act's definition — depriving a person of occupation against their will — and section 8(3) makes it an offence carrying a fine or imprisonment not exceeding two years, or both. It is separately an unfair practice, and the Tribunal can order spoliation.

What this means for your file

Everything above is proved from documents you either have or do not: the lease with its term and breach clause; which state it is in today; the letter of demand with its delivery date and proof of receipt; the notice of cancellation, dated after the statutory period had run; and the arrears as at cancellation. Where this needs an attorney

All of it, in truth: an eviction is litigation, and nothing here substitutes for instructing someone. Three points especially. Whether your termination was valid, since that decides whether there is an unlawful occupier at all. Whether section 4(6) or 4(7) applies, and how the six months is computed for a long-standing tenant whose occupation only recently became unlawful. And any case where a lockout or disconnection has already happened, because section 8 makes that criminal as well as civil.

This guide is general information about the Prevention of Illegal Eviction from and Unlawful Occupation of Land Act 19 of 1998 and related legislation as commonly applied to South African residential tenancies. It is not legal advice. Eviction is a court process; procedure differs between the magistrate's court and the High Court and between divisions, and whether eviction is just and equitable turns on the facts and the court's discretion. No provision of the Act guarantees a timeline. Instruct an attorney before terminating a lease you intend to enforce by eviction, and before any step in the proceedings themselves.
