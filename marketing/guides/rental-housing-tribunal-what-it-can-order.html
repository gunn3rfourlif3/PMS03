The Rental Housing Tribunal: what it can order, and what it cannot

Most agencies meet the Tribunal the same way: a letter arrives, nobody is quite sure whether it is a court, and somebody suggests phoning an attorney. The answer is worth knowing in advance, because the Tribunal is free, its rulings are enforced as magistrate's court orders, and for one large category of dispute you do not get to choose a court instead. Here is what it can order, the two things it cannot, and what changes the day a complaint is lodged.

A provincial body, not a court, with a court's teeth

Section 7 of the Rental Housing Act 50 of 1999 provides for an MEC to establish a Rental Housing Tribunal in the province by notice in the Gazette. They are provincial bodies, with provincial forms, offices and capacity — so the process you learned in one province is not quite the process in the next. Section 8 then requires the Tribunal to do all things necessary to achieve the objectives of Chapter 4, a broader mandate than a court has, and the reason it investigates rather than waiting to be persuaded.

The part that matters commercially is section 13(13): a ruling by the Tribunal is deemed to be an order of a magistrate's court in terms of the Magistrates' Courts Act 32 of 1944, and is enforced under that Act. It is not a recommendation. Any tenant, landlord, group of either, or interest group may lodge a complaint under section 13(1), and both the Western Cape and Gauteng Tribunals describe the service as free from lodging through to the end of mediation or a hearing.

Everything turns on "unfair practice"

The Tribunal's jurisdiction is not general. It hears complaints about an unfair practice, and that phrase decides whether a dispute belongs to it or to a court.

The content comes largely from provincial regulations made under section 15. The Western Cape's Unfair Practices Regulations, published as Provincial Notice 22/2002 on 1 February 2002, show how concrete this gets: regulation 2 makes a failure to comply with the regulations itself an unfair practice, and the regulations cover changing locks, habitability and maintenance, entry to the dwelling, written receipts and municipal services — some with hard numbers, such as repairs within 30 days of the inspection or notice unless a longer period is agreed, and electricity or gas cut for arrears only after seven days' notice. Other provinces have their own regulations, and this is one of the places where "it varies by province" is not a hedge.

Two points of law widen the net further than agencies expect. In Maphango and Others v Aengus Lifestyle Properties (Pty) Ltd [2012] ZACC 2, decided on 13 March 2012, the Constitutional Court held that a "practice" may consist in a single act, rejecting the Supreme Court of Appeal's view that it had to be systemic. The same judgment read section 4(5)(c) conjunctively: a landlord may terminate only on grounds that both do not constitute an unfair practice and are specified in the lease — so a termination the lease plainly permits can still be an unfair practice, and that is the Tribunal's call. The Court postponed the eviction appeal so the tenants could take the question to the Gauteng Tribunal first.

Then there is section 13(9): from the date a Tribunal is established in the province, any dispute about an unfair practice must be determined by the Tribunal, unless proceedings have already been instituted in another court — and section 13(11) lets a magistrate's court refer such a dispute to the Tribunal at any time. Which is why the forum question is worth settling at the start rather than after a summons.

What a ruling can actually do

More than most principals assume. Under section 13(4) the Tribunal may rule that a party comply with a provision of the Act, refer an apparent contravention of another law to the competent body, and make any just and fair ruling to terminate an unfair practice — the Act's own examples being overcrowding, unacceptable living conditions, exploitative rentals and lack of maintenance.

Section 13(5) goes further and allows a rent determination, which must be just and equitable to both parties and take into account prevailing economic conditions and the need for a realistic return for investors.

Section 13(12) adds the remedies that catch agencies off guard: a costs ruling as may be just and equitable, the power to make a mediation agreement a ruling, and the power to issue spoliation and attachment orders and grant interdicts. A spoliation order is the answer to a landlord who changed the locks — and here it comes from a free provincial tribunal, not a High Court application.

The two things it cannot do

First, it cannot evict. Section 13(14) says so plainly, and both the Gauteng and Western Cape Tribunals repeat it in their own material. Eviction is a court application under the Prevention of Illegal Eviction from and Unlawful Occupation of Land Act 19 of 1998, and nothing the Tribunal rules shortens it.

Second, it cannot presently order a tenant to pay rent. A power to compel payment of rent specified in a lease and of arrear rentals was inserted into section 13(12) by the Rental Housing Amendment Act 35 of 2014 — and that Act, gazetted on 5 November 2014, still takes effect from a date to be proclaimed. Until proclamation, arrear rent recovery is a court matter, which section 13(10) expressly preserves along with urgent relief and eviction where no unfair practice dispute exists.

What changes the day a complaint is lodged

Section 13(7) is the provision to know by heart. From the date a complaint is lodged until the Tribunal rules or three months elapse, whichever is earlier: the landlord may not evict the tenant; the tenant must keep paying the rent payable before the complaint, or, if there was an escalation immediately before it, the pre-escalation amount; and the landlord must effect necessary maintenance.

Read that second limb again, because it is the one that costs money: a complaint lodged shortly after an increase can roll the rent back to the prior figure for up to three months, and an agency that cannot show when the escalation took effect, and on what written basis, is arguing from memory. The Western Cape Tribunal tells complainants a matter may take up to 90 days to finalise, which lines up with the same three months.

Why it is usually faster — and the catch

The speed comes from the structure. There is no filing fee, no pleadings and no attorney required to start. Section 13(2) has the Tribunal investigate, appoint a mediator where mediation may resolve the dispute, and hold a formal hearing only if mediation is unsuitable or fails. A mediated agreement can then be made a ruling under section 13(12)(b), which converts a settlement into something enforceable without a second process.

The catch is the exit. There is no appeal on the merits: section 17 provides that Tribunal proceedings may be brought under review before the High Court, and review is about how the decision was made, not whether it was right. The appeal to the MEC sits in the 2014 Amendment Act and is not in force, so a ruling you dislike is, in practical terms, one you live with. Non-compliance is an offence too: section 16 covers failing to comply with a ruling under section 13(4), and unlawfully locking out a tenant or shutting off utilities, with a convicted person liable to a fine or imprisonment not exceeding two years, or both.

What this means for your file

Almost every unfair practice category above is proved or disproved by one document: the lease with its escalation basis and date, the deposit and its interest, the two joint inspection reports, dated maintenance requests and what was done about each, receipts and statements, and municipal readings where you bill services on. A lock change agreed on the phone and never written down is what turns a recoverable arrears matter into a spoliation order against the landlord. Our guide on rental deposits under the Rental Housing Act covers the inspection records the Tribunal asks for most often.

Where this needs an attorney

Four situations: any eviction, which is a court process and not a Tribunal one; a complaint where you are unsure whether the dispute is an unfair practice at all, since section 13(9) may take the choice of forum away from you; a ruling you are considering taking on review, which is a High Court application on narrow grounds; and any matter where a lockout or utility disconnection has already happened, because section 16 makes that a criminal question as well as a civil one.

This guide is general information about Chapter 4 of the Rental Housing Act 50 of 1999 and the provincial unfair practice regulations as commonly applied to South African rental agencies. It is not legal advice. Tribunals are provincial and their regulations, forms, procedures and capacity differ by province; whether particular conduct is an unfair practice turns on the facts; and the Rental Housing Amendment Act 35 of 2014 would change several of the powers described above if and when it is brought into operation — check its status before relying on it. Before responding to a complaint, or on any eviction, speak to an attorney.
