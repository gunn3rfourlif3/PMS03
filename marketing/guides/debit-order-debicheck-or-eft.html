Debit order, DebiCheck or EFT: how rent collection methods actually compare

Most agencies did not choose a collection method. They inherited one, and it works until the month it does not — a tenant disputes four debits at once, or the auditor asks why last month's rent sat in somebody else's bank account for three days. The four methods differ in one respect that matters far more than the per-transaction price: who holds the mandate, and who carries the reversal.

Four ways the rent arrives

The tenant pushes the money. A manual EFT or a PayShap payment. You hold no mandate, so there is nothing for a bank to reverse — and you control nothing: not the date, not the amount, not the reference. The real cost is reconciliation: a payment referenced with the tenant's surname is an unallocated receipt until somebody works it out by hand.

EFT debit order. The oldest stream and still the most common. The mandate is an agreement between you and the tenant, held by you and not registered with their bank. PASA describes EFT debits as processed in the late evening window, after the Registered Mandate stream, with no tracking. If the tenant disputes one, the bank reverses it without determining whether it was valid.

Registered Mandate (RM). Launched on 12 May 2025 in place of the legacy Registered Mandate Service. The mandate is registered with the tenant's bank, carrying the contract reference and your contact details, and the bank notifies the tenant once it is registered — the tenant is told, not asked. RM runs in the evening window ahead of EFT and carries credit tracking for up to ten days, which matters when a tenant is paid late.

DebiCheck. The tenant confirms the mandate electronically with their own bank when the lease starts, and the bank then checks each collection against its own record of what was agreed. Collections run in the earliest morning window, directly after bulk credits — ahead of the salary-day queue — and a valid, authorised DebiCheck collection is not disputable.

What changed on 13 April 2026

From 13 April 2026 the period within which a debit order can be disputed fell from 365 days to 60, and the separate manual dispute process that applied after 40 days fell away. An EFT debit disputed inside the 60 days is reversed immediately; past 60 days the tenant has to take the matter up with you directly. The Reserve Bank and the Financial Sector Conduct Authority confirmed the change, and banks have published it to customers in those terms.

Read it both ways. Your tail risk on a legacy EFT book is now two months rather than a year. But inside those 60 days the reversal is automatic, and the mandate you rely on is a document in your file rather than a record at the bank — so you argue the point after the money has left the trust account. On a valid DebiCheck mandate there is nothing to argue about: the bank already holds the authorisation.

DebiCheck is friction at signing in exchange for finality later

Providers refer to three mandate request types, labelled TT1, TT2 and TT3: a real-time request the tenant approves in the moment, a batched request with a short approval window, and one authenticated with a bank card and PIN on a point-of-sale device. The mechanics differ; the cost to you is the same, and it is onboarding friction. A request not approved in time has to be sent again — in practice, to a tenant who has left your office and stopped answering. So obtain the mandate during the signing appointment, with the tenant's phone in their hand, and treat an unapproved mandate as an incomplete lease file. The early-morning slot belongs to this stream in any event: the Reserve Bank's position from 1 May 2021 was that all new and renegotiated early debit order mandates originate in the authenticated collections system.

The protection has one condition, and it is the one agencies lose: you must collect within the terms the tenant authenticated. An escalation that goes through before the mandate is amended, or arrears loaded onto the rent line, is a collection the mandate does not cover — and the finality goes with it. Hence: keep arrears recovery off the rent collection, and amend the mandate in the month the escalation lands, which is one more reason to know what your escalation clause does on renewal.

What the bureau is actually vetting

You will not collect directly; you go through a bureau, and that bureau sits inside a regulated structure. PASA expects anyone processing payment instructions for two or more clients to seek system operator authorisation, and makes it compulsory above 10 000 transactions a month or a combined value above R10 million a month, under section 4(2)(c) of the National Payment System Act 78 of 1998, with criteria aligned to the Reserve Bank's System Operator Directive 2 of 2007. A bureau collecting on your behalf also registers as a third party payment provider through a sponsoring bank under Directive 1 of 2007 — PASA charges no fee for that and gives 21 working days once the bank has submitted everything.

That structure explains the onboarding questions. The bureau is answerable upward for what it submits, so it vets you before your first run: company and FICA documents, verification of the account collections settle into, and evidence of how you obtain mandates and where you keep them. Expect to be asked to produce a given tenant's mandate on demand, with a date and a method. Commercial terms and security requirements differ by bureau and sponsoring bank — get them in writing.

Where the money may land

Settle this before the first run, not after the first audit. Section 54(1)(a) of the Property Practitioners Act 22 of 2019 requires a separate trust account, containing a reference to that section, at a bank registered under the Banks Act, and section 54(1)(d) requires the practitioner to immediately deposit all trust money held or received by or on behalf of that practitioner into it. Collected rent is trust money, and "on behalf of" is doing work in that sentence: a bureau that collects into its own account and settles to you some days later is holding trust money on your behalf for that period. Whether your arrangement is acceptable is a question for your auditor and for the bureau's contract, and the answer is far easier when settlement goes directly into the trust account, one deposit per run, reconciling line by line to the run's own report. Section 54(5)(b) then requires the books to be balanced at intervals of not more than one month, and none of that gets easier with a settlement you cannot reconcile — which is the argument of our guide on why a trust audit costs more than it should, on top of the trust account rules themselves.

The receipt you still owe the tenant

Automating the collection does not remove the receipt. Section 5(3)(a) of the Rental Housing Act 50 of 1999 requires the landlord to furnish the tenant with a written receipt for all payments received, and section 5(3)(b) is specific: dated, clearly indicating the address including the street number and any further description of the dwelling, whether payment was for rental, arrears, deposit or otherwise, and the period it covers. Section 5(4) provides that these standard provisions may not be waived, so a clause in which the tenant accepts a bank statement instead buys nothing — and a bureau's debit order confirmation is not that receipt either. Generate it off the collection run, per tenant, monthly.

What this means for your file

A defensible position for a small agency: new leases onto DebiCheck, mandate obtained at signing; RM where a DebiCheck mandate cannot be had but you want it registered and tracked; EFT debit for the legacy book only, migrated at renewal; manual payment for the handful who will not mandate, each with a unique reference. Per tenant the file carries the mandate with its date and method, the bank's confirmation where it is DebiCheck, the authenticated amount and date checked against the current rent, and the monthly receipt. On that file an unpaid is an unpaid — a collections problem with a next step — not an argument about whether you were entitled to collect at all.

Where this needs an attorney

Three situations. A mandate amendment for an escalation, or to consolidate arrears into the collection. A bureau contract with funds settling anywhere other than directly into your trust account. And a tenant disputing a run of collections on the basis that no valid mandate existed, because that is an allegation about your records rather than about the rent. A reversed debit order is neither a cancelled lease nor a ground for self-help: the arrear is recovered as an arrear, through the lease, the deposit at termination, and where it becomes a dispute, the forum described in our guide on what the Tribunal can order.

It is not legal advice. Payment system rules are made by the payments industry body and its participating banks rather than by statute, and they change: the dispute changes described above took effect on 13 April 2026, and current scheme rules, value limits and dispute grounds should be confirmed with your sponsoring bank or bureau before you rely on them. We do not quote per-transaction pricing, bureau security requirements or stream value limits here because they vary by provider and are not publishable as general figures. There is no published switch-off date for the EFT debit stream as at the date of this guide, so treat migration as commercially advisable rather than as a deadline. The Rental Housing Amendment Act 35 of 2014 would renumber and alter parts of section 5 if and when it is brought into operation. On a mandate amendment, a settlement arrangement or a disputed run, speak to an attorney, and raise trust account structure with your auditor.
