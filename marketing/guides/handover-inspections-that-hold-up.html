Handover inspections that hold up: the two joint inspections, and the finding that survives a dispute

A deposit dispute is not won at the Tribunal. It is won or lost on two mornings — one before the tenant moves in, one in the last days of the lease — and on whether whoever held the clipboard wrote it down in a form that still makes sense a year later. The Act is blunt about the cost of getting it wrong.

Skip either inspection and you forfeit the lot

Section 5(3) of the Rental Housing Act 50 of 1999 requires two joint inspections. Under paragraph (e), tenant and landlord must jointly inspect the dwelling before the tenant moves in, to record any defects or damage. Under paragraph (f), they must arrange a joint inspection at a mutually convenient time within a period of three days prior to the expiration of the lease, to establish what damage occurred during the tenancy.

Paragraph (j) is the one principals underestimate. A failure by the landlord to inspect is deemed to be an acknowledgement that the dwelling is in a good and proper state of repair: the landlord has no further claim against the tenant and must refund the full deposit together with interest. Its trigger is the failure to inspect, and that duty sits in both (e) and (f) — so missing the inspection before move-in can cost you the same deposit as missing the one at the end. Most agencies do not rank them that way, though the incoming schedule is also the only thing the outgoing one can be measured against.

Nor can you contract out of it: section 5(4) provides that these standard provisions may not be waived by either party, so a clause in which the tenant takes the property "as is" and dispenses with the incoming inspection buys nothing.

The three days are the Act's, and the window moves

Paragraph (f) puts the outgoing inspection "within a period of three days prior to such expiration". On its plain wording that means inside the final three days of the lease rather than three or more days before the end — though it is read the other way in practice, and the point does not appear to be settled. Either way the window is days, not weeks, and the arranging is the landlord's job — so invite the tenant in writing before it opens, and keep proof.

Three refund periods then run, and not from the same event. Where nothing is owed, paragraph (i) requires the full deposit plus interest within seven days of expiration, with no deductions. Where the inspection was held and lawful deductions are made, paragraph (g) requires the balance not later than 14 days of restoration of the dwelling to the landlord — restoration, not expiration, so a tenant who hands back keys late moves that date. Where the tenant fails to respond, paragraph (k) requires you to inspect within seven days of expiration, (l) preserves the deduction for reasonable repair and lost-key costs, and (m) requires the balance not later than 21 days after expiration.

The window can also arrive early: section 14 of the Consumer Protection Act 68 of 2008 lets a consumer cancel a fixed-term agreement on 20 business days' written notice.

Before the incoming inspection, the disclosure form

The Property Practitioners Act 22 of 2019, in force since 1 February 2022, adds a step ahead of the Rental Housing Act's inspection, and it applies to lettings, not only sales. Section 67(1)(a) prohibits a practitioner from accepting a mandate unless the lessor has provided a fully completed and signed mandatory disclosure in the prescribed form; section 67(1)(b) requires a copy to go to a prospective lessee intending to make an offer.

Section 67(2) is where it bites: the completed form, signed by all relevant parties, must be attached to the agreement, and where it is not, the agreement must be interpreted as if no defects or deficiencies of the property were disclosed. That is a second deeming provision pointing the same way as section 5(3)(j): a defect the owner knew about and the tenant accepted becomes, on the paper you will be holding, a defect nobody mentioned. Section 67(3) adds that a non-complying practitioner may be held liable by an affected consumer, and the regulations treat the breach as a minor contravention carrying a prescribed penalty.

Note too that section 5(3)(d) still carves out registered estate agents by reference to the repealed Estate Agency Affairs Act 112 of 1976; what governs an agency holding a deposit now is the PPRA trust-account regime.

What to photograph, and what a photograph is for

A photograph is not evidence of damage. It is evidence of condition on a date, which is narrower and more useful. Shoot for comparison, not for grievance.

Every room wide from the doorway, so the later set can be taken from the same spot. Each recorded defect close, with something in frame for scale. Meter readings — electricity, water, the prepaid balance — photographed rather than transcribed, because somebody will query them. The geyser, distribution board, stove and visible plumbing — the expensive arguments. Keys, remotes and access cards laid out and counted in one frame, the cheapest way to settle the lost-key deduction paragraph (g) allows. The exterior, pool, garden and gate motor where the lease makes them somebody's responsibility.

Keep the originals rather than copies pasted into a document, which lose their capture dates, and store them against the lease. At the outgoing inspection, work the same list in the same order: a set that cannot be lined up against the incoming one cannot make the argument.

A finding that holds up is a sentence, not a word

"Kitchen — damaged" loses. A finding that survives carries five things: where it is, down to the location rather than the room; the item; the observed condition in neutral language; the incoming entry it is measured against, quoted, including where that entry reads "no defect recorded"; and what is proposed. "Kitchen, cupboard door below sink: lower hinge pulled out of the carcass, door hanging. Incoming schedule 14/03/2025 records no defect for this item. Replace hinge, plug and redrill screw holes" is a finding. The first is a note to self.

Record the tenant's comment in their own words, and have them sign the schedule including the lines they dispute — a recorded disagreement is worth more than a signature on a page nobody read, because it shows the inspection was genuinely joint. Then price it. Paragraph (g) permits the reasonable cost of repairing damage during the lease period; reasonable is proved with invoices, and paragraph (n) requires those receipts to be available for the tenant to inspect. A quote is not a receipt — and if the work cannot be done before the applicable 14 or 21 days expire, that is a sequencing problem to solve inside the notice period, not a pricing one afterwards.

Fair wear and tear is the argument you will actually have

The phrase does not appear in section 5 at all. It appears in section 4(5)(d)(i), among the landlord's rights on termination: to receive the property in a good state of repair, save for fair wear and tear. The Act does not define it, so a deduction schedule is a set of judgements rather than a calculation.

What moves it is the length of the tenancy, the expected life of the item, and whether the condition is what ordinary use produces. Three years of traffic flattening a carpet is wear; a burn in it is damage. Where you cannot honestly place a line, leave it off — one overreaching item is the usual reason a tenant challenges a schedule that was otherwise sound, and the Tribunal then looks at all of it.

What this means for your file

Per lease the bundle either exists or it does not: the disclosure form, signed and attached; the incoming schedule, dated and signed, with its photographs; the written invitation to the outgoing inspection and proof of delivery; the outgoing schedule with the tenant's comments and signature, or the record of non-response and the date you inspected alone; the deduction schedule tied item by item to the incoming baseline, with its invoices; the interest statement; and the refund, paid inside whichever of the 7, 14 or 21 day periods applies. Our guide on rental deposits covers the interest and the deadlines, and what the Tribunal can order covers where a thin bundle ends up.

Where this needs an attorney

Three situations. Where the amount is material and the real dispute is whether a condition is damage or fair wear and tear, a factual argument with no statutory answer. Where the tenant says they were never invited to the outgoing inspection, because section 5(3)(j) turns on that one question and the consequence is the whole deposit. And where the lease ended early or irregularly, since paragraph (g) runs from restoration of the dwelling while (i) and (m) run from expiration.

This guide is general information about section 5 of the Rental Housing Act 50 of 1999 and section 67 of the Property Practitioners Act 22 of 2019 as commonly applied to South African residential tenancies. It is not legal advice. Fair wear and tear is not defined in the Act and turns on the facts of each case; the timing wording in section 5(3)(f) is read differently by different practitioners; provincial unfair practice regulations add detail that varies by province; and the Rental Housing Amendment Act 35 of 2014 would renumber and alter several of the provisions described above if and when it is brought into operation — check its status before relying on it. On a live deposit dispute, speak to an attorney or your provincial Rental Housing Tribunal.
