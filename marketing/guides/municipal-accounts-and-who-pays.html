Municipal accounts and who pays: recovering utilities without breaching the Act

Rent is the easy part of a letting agency's collections. The municipal account is where the money goes missing — because the person the municipality will pursue is almost never the person consuming the electricity, and because the obvious way to close that gap, a charge on top, is the one thing a tribunal has already struck down.

The municipality's debtor is the owner

Start here, because most of the bad decisions downstream come from getting it wrong. Whatever the lease says, a tenant's undertaking to pay for services is an undertaking to the landlord: it does not make the tenant the municipality's debtor, and it does not move the risk.

The Constitutional Court settled that in Mkontwana v Nelson Mandela Metropolitan Municipality 2005 (1) SA 530 (CC). Section 118(1) of the Local Government: Municipal Systems Act 32 of 2000 bars a registrar of deeds from registering a transfer without a prescribed certificate covering amounts that became due in connection with the property during the two years preceding the date of application for it. The Court held the resulting deprivation of property was not arbitrary: the section "requires the owner of the property to bear the risk of non-payment" by a non-owner occupier, because the owner chooses the occupier, sets the payment terms and can require proof of payment.

Section 118(3) adds that an amount due for municipal service fees, rates and other municipal taxes, levies and duties "is a charge upon the property in connection with which the amount is owing", preferent to any registered mortgage bond — and rates are the owner's to begin with. So the position is blunt: the consumption is the tenant's, the exposure is the owner's, and your job is to make the two line up monthly rather than find the gap at transfer.

What you may recharge, and the ceiling on it

Where the dwelling is separately metered, the provincial unfair practice regulations say the same thing in substance: the tenant pays what the meter in that dwelling records, and nothing else. The Western Cape version (Rental Housing Unfair Practices Regulations, Western Cape, 2002, P.N. 22/2002) frames it as a duty not to charge a tenant more than the exact services consumed in that tenant's separately metered dwelling. Where it is not separately metered you are into apportionment, and in a multi-tenanted building the total recovered may not exceed what the service provider charged.

The margin is not yours to take. In Young Ming Shan CC v Chagan NO and Others 2015 (3) SA 227 (GJ) a Johannesburg landlord levied a monthly electricity "service charge" of about R385 per unit on top of each tenant's consumption, across roughly eighty tenants, when the building's City Power bill was a fraction of what that collected. The Gauteng Rental Housing Tribunal found an unfair practice, barred the charge and ordered repayment, and the High Court upheld it on review: a charge set at the landlord's discretion can be used to recover losses from non-paying tenants or simply to profit, which is unfair to the tenants who do pay. Administrative costs belong in the rent, where a tenant sees them before signing.

Selling electricity on also makes you a reseller, and the exemption resellers rely on — Schedule 2 to the Electricity Regulation Act 4 of 2006 — was, in the version gazetted on 12 August 2021, conditional on the price not exceeding the tariff the distributor would have charged, on registration with the Regulator, and on a ratified service delivery agreement. Schedule 2 has been substituted more than once since, so check the version in force.

The statement the tenant is entitled to

Where a separately metered tenant pays you rather than the municipality, the provincial regulations require a monthly statement, and are specific about it. The Western Cape list runs to the previous and current month's meter readings, the actual consumption for each service and the amounts charged for it, the date of the next reading, and any arrears. Section 5(6)(h) of the Act separately requires the lease to state the rental and any other charges payable in addition to it, so "plus utilities" is not a charging clause.

Be careful what you are reading, though. A detailed set of national Procedural and Unfair Practice Regulations dated 2008 circulates widely and is quoted as law on a good number of property sites, but it was published as a draft for comment — Notice 340 of 2008, Government Gazette 30863, 14 March 2008. What binds you is the regulations in force in your province, and they differ.

You cannot switch the supply off

This is what turns a collections problem into a criminal one. Section 16(hA) of the Rental Housing Act makes it an offence to unlawfully lock out a tenant or shut off the utilities to the rental housing property. The word doing the work is "unlawfully", and what makes an interruption lawful is not the size of the arrear but a specific authority to interrupt.

The Western Cape regulations, for instance, permit interruption of electricity or gas in an emergency, for maintenance or repairs where supply resumes on completion, or where the tenant is in arrears with the service fees and fails to pay within seven days of a notice to do so. Other provinces are drafted differently. Water is separate: section 4(3) of the Water Services Act 108 of 1997 requires the procedures for limiting or discontinuing it to be fair and equitable, with reasonable notice and an opportunity to make representations, and they may not deny anyone basic water services for non-payment where that person proves inability to pay. Disconnection at the meter generally belongs to the licensed distributor in any event.

So arrears get recovered as arrears — through the lease, the deposit at termination, and where the dispute is an unfair practice, the forum described in our guide on what the Tribunal can order. Self-help at the distribution board turns your client's claim into the tenant's complaint.

An account in arrears at handover

You cannot reconcile what you have not seen, and you are entitled to see it: section 102(3) of the Municipal Systems Act requires a municipality, on the owner's written request, to furnish copies of the accounts sent to the occupier for municipal services. Make that request once, in writing, per property, at the start of the mandate — and note that section 102(1) cuts the other way, letting a municipality consolidate a person's accounts and credit a payment against any of them, so paying the rates while refuse removal runs behind ring-fences nothing.

Where the account is already behind, the arrear is not the incoming tenant's. Get the balance in writing and date it. Read and photograph every meter on the day, so the opening reading is the boundary between somebody else's consumption and your tenant's — the same discipline as the incoming schedule in our guide on handover inspections. Then tell the owner in writing what the exposure is, because diligent monthly recharging does not cure a historical balance.

At transfer, two rules meet. Section 118(1) blocks registration until amounts that became due in the two years preceding the certificate application are paid, so a clearance certificate does not prove the account is clean — only that the two-year window is settled. For what lies behind it, Jordaan and Others v City of Tshwane Metropolitan Municipality [2017] ZACC 31 held that section 118(3), properly interpreted, does not make historical debt enforceable against a new owner who takes transfer, though it stands against the owner who incurred the debt. Commentary written before August 2017 says the opposite, and plenty of it is still online.

What this means for your file

Per property: the section 102(3) request and the account copies it produces; the metering position at the start of the mandate, with opening readings and photographs; a lease stating each service charge as section 5(6)(h) requires, with no discretionary fee on top; a monthly statement to every separately metered tenant carrying readings, consumption, amounts and arrears; and the municipal invoice behind each recharge. On that file the recharge reconciles to the municipal account line by line — which is what makes it defensible at the Tribunal and uneventful at trust audit.

Where this needs an attorney

Three situations. Where a building is not separately metered and you need an apportionment method that will survive an unfair practice complaint. Where you recharge electricity at anything other than the distributor's tariff, or add a fee to it, because that is a reseller question under Schedule 2 rather than a lease question. And where an account was in arrears at handover and the owner's instruction is to recover it from the tenant.

This guide is general information about sections 102 and 118 of the Local Government: Municipal Systems Act 32 of 2000, sections 5, 15 and 16 of the Rental Housing Act 50 of 1999, section 4 of the Water Services Act 108 of 1997 and Schedule 2 to the Electricity Regulation Act 4 of 2006. It is not legal advice. Unfair practice regulations are made provincially and differ materially: the Western Cape provisions quoted above are illustrative, not national, and Young Ming Shan turned on the Gauteng regulations. Schedule 2 has been substituted more than once since 2021, and municipal electricity and water by-laws add local requirements. The Rental Housing Amendment Act 35 of 2014 would renumber and alter several of the provisions described above if and when it is brought into operation — check its status before relying on it. On a live arrear, a disconnection or an apportionment dispute, speak to an attorney or your provincial Rental Housing Tribunal.

The recharge that reconciles to the municipal account

Locare records the meters and opening readings against the property, recharges metered consumption at the tariff with the municipal invoice attached, and issues the monthly tenant statement with readings, consumption and arrears on it — so what you billed and what the municipality billed are the same conversation.
